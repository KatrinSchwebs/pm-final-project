# Competitive Analysis & Journey Map (Module 2)

## Responses
- **Role, who are you solving for? (the specific user segment or profile):** Mara
- **Goal, what is this user ultimately trying to achieve?:** Goal: Mara wants to settle her open claim(s) on terms she can actually afford and keep, so she can put the matter behind her without damaging her finances or having to call anyone.
- **Friction, the main barrier (moment of misery) stopping them from succeeding:** Friction: Mara reaches the income and affordability step without knowing why she’s being asked, what happens if she misses a payment, or whether it could make things worse. She doesn’t trust the collector to tell her, so she leaves the journey to search online, ask a debt adviser or family, work out a number in a spreadsheet, or simply do nothing. The ones who push on end up either calling an agent or signing up for a plan they guessed at and can’t keep.
- **External tools, the outside platforms or tools the user is forced to use:** Mara nutzt vor allem drei Arten von Hilfsmitteln, weil das Portal ihr an der entscheidenden Stelle nicht weiterhilft:

Information. Sie googelt Fragen wie „Ist dieser Brief seriös?“, „Kann ich in Raten zahlen?“ und „Was passiert, wenn ich eine Rate verpasse?“. Zusätzlich besucht sie Verbraucherportale oder wendet sich an eine Schuldnerberatung, weil sie dem Inkassounternehmen nicht zutraut, sie neutral zu beraten. Familie und Freunde fragt sie meist nur zur Beruhigung (abgeleitet, der Hook nennt keinen konkreten Kanal).

Berechnung. Sie rechnet ihre Leistbarkeit selbst aus, in einer Tabellenkalkulation (z. B. Excel oder Google Sheets) oder in ihrer Banking-App, und schätzt dann einen Monatsbetrag. Ihre echten Zahlen liegen dort, aber nichts verbindet sie mit dem Ratenzahlungsprozess (abgeleitet).

Prüfung der Forderung. Sie durchsucht ihr E-Mail-Postfach und ihre Handyfotos nach Rechnung, Vertrag oder früherer Korrespondenz, um zu kontrollieren, ob die Forderung stimmt. Das Portal zeigt ihr offenbar nicht genug Details, um das direkt zu klären (Fotos: abgeleitet).

Als letzten Ausweg ruft sie die Telefonhotline von Riverty an. Das ist das einzige Hilfsmittel, das Riverty selbst gehört, und genau der Kanal, den die Initiative reduzieren soll.
- **The process, the 3 to 5 manual steps the user takes to get the job done:** 1. Check whether the claim is real.
She searches her inbox and photos for the original invoice or earlier letters, and googles “is this letter legitimate.” The portal doesn’t give her enough detail to confirm it on the spot, so she verifies elsewhere.

2. Look for neutral advice.
She searches “can I pay in instalments” and “what if I miss a payment,” reads consumer advice sites, and may contact a debt counsellor or ask family and friends. She does this because she doesn’t trust the collector to answer these questions honestly.

3. Work out what she can afford.
She builds a rough budget in a spreadsheet or her banking app and picks a monthly amount. Nothing in the journey helps her test that number, so it is a guess.

4. Return to the portal and try to fit her answer in.
She enters her guessed amount and income details, often without knowing why they’re asked or what happens if she misses a payment. This is the point where some drop out silently.

5. Commit or escalate.
She either signs up for the plan she guessed at, which she may not be able to keep, or gives up and calls an agent, repeating information she may already have entered.
- **Core frustration, the exact moment the process feels most “broken”:** The moment it feels most broken: step 4, when she returns to the portal with her own number and hits the income and affordability question.

By then she has already done the hard work herself. She has checked the claim, looked for neutral advice and built a budget. She comes back ready to commit, and the portal asks her for income details without saying why, what happens to that information, or what happens if she misses a payment. She has no way to test the amount she’s about to enter, and nobody she trusts is on the screen to ask.

In her words (illustrative, not a research quote):

“I did all the work to figure out what I can pay. Now it asks for my income and won’t tell me why, or what happens if I get it wrong.”

Why this is the breaking point and not an earlier step:

Steps 1 to 3 are annoying but self-directed. She’s in control and making progress.
At step 4 she is asked to hand over sensitive information and commit to a number, with no explanation. That is where the stakes are highest and the support is lowest.
It’s also the point of no return in the journey. From here she either commits to a guess, calls an agent, or leaves silently.

What makes it feel broken rather than just hard: the portal asks her to trust it at exactly the moment she has least reason to. The trust gap from your hook (she doesn’t believe the collector will advise her neutrally) collides with a form that demands proof of what she can pay.
- **The evidence, a specific quote or behavior from the research that proves this:** Mehrfachforderungs-Kunden werden vom Portal strukturell benachteiligt (T-202: keine gemeinsame Planung, N-03: „Ich konnte nur eine Forderung auf einmal sehen“), und das zeigt sich in einer Abschlussquote von 27,6 % bei drei oder mehr Forderungen gegenüber 52,1 % bei einer Forderung, wobei der Beleg dafür fast nur in den Kennzahlen und dem Ticket liegt und kaum in den Interviews.
